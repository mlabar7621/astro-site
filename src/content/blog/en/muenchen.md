Among all the places I have lived for more than a week thus far in life, Munich has been the nicest.

As a tourist, or as anyone who only ever sees the very center of the city, I would understand the impression of Munich being somewhat lackluster. Everything is geared towards tourists: the highway robbery in shops and restaurants, the overabundance of museums dedicated to the most menial of things, the historic church at every corner. After my first week there, most of which was spent in the center, I did not have a positive impression of the city. Nevertheless, even for short stays, there are two very high praises I do have for the city: public museums are cheap and **really good**, and everything feels safe. Even wandering down alleys near Central Station (Hauptbahnhof) at midnight, I never felt like I was genuinely at risk of being robbed. That is something I can not say about Paris or Hamburg.

Having lived in Munich for 6 months during a study abroad program, though, my views on the city could hardly be better.

I lived in Olydorf during my study abroad program, so I was uniquely lucky to pay a token amount for my apartment. I know that most are not so lucky and will be paying 1000/month or more for even a dormitory. I also lived remarkably close to Olympiapark, a massive city park host to tons of events. The nearest metro station, too, was not more than a short walk away. As such, I feel like I truly got to see the best that the city has to offer. Your particular mileage may vary, but you too will be able to see and do everything available if you just put in a bit more of your time.

Firstly, transportation links are excellent. Germany does not have the greatest reputation for train timeliness, but I think Bavaria is an exception. Metro, trams, S-Bahn, and regional trains were all generally on-time and nice. The schedules were updated on google maps and tickets were pretty easy to buy, though day and week tickets were very expensive; you really need to buy a month or more worth of rides to get an acceptable deal. With that in mind, you can get just about anywhere via public transportation. The tiniest villages and most remote mountains are rarely, if ever, more than a ten minute bike ride from the nearest stop of some kind. During my six months, I did not need to get into a car even once.

You will notice that I excluded buses in my list. In my experience, they were not at all up to par in comparison to the other modes of transport. Sometimes they arrived a few minutes late, which was fine, but sometimes they never arrived at all. I once waited at a stop for 45 minutes for a bus that should have been running every 15; there were no delays listed on the MVG app and no info written on the station sign. I can only assume that the bus got into a traffic accident or something. Also, buses sometimes left early. THat may seem better than leaving late, but in practice, when I have just a few minutes to switch from metro to bus and I miss my line because the bus left two minutes early, that equates to a 15-20 minute delay. I would rather get somewhere two minutes late than twenty minutes late. They were also not as civil as the other modes of transport. Not once did I see a tweaker or crazy person on the metro or tram, but I seemed to see one on the bus almost eveyr week. Generally, I would avoid buses when possible.

Secondly, entertainment was plentiful and easily accessible. That includes more high-brow stuff: museums, classical music, trade fairs, plays, and opera, as well as more commonplace stuff like sports games, rock concerts, cultural festivals, bars, and clubs. I was able to attend at least one of each for no more than 25 per ticket. It was genuinely incredible to me how much variety was offered. There were museums and memorials for everything. The Bavarian National Museum had an incredible collection of porcelain and furniture. The Deutsches Museum was the best science museum I have ever visited.
